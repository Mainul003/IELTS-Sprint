🎧 Listening — Section 4

Survival tactics

  • Section 4 is one uninterrupted lecture — no dialogue cues to re-orient you. Pre-read all 10 gaps the moment Section 3 ends; predict the word type (number? name? noun?) for each before audio starts.
  • The talk follows your note sheet in strict order. If you miss a blank, don't backtrack — jump to the next signpost and keep moving.
  • Listen for signpost language that flags an answer is coming: "the first factor is…", "in contrast…", "this led to…", "more importantly…".
  • Self-corrections are a trap: if the speaker says "in 1990 — well, actually 1991" the second value is the answer.
  • After writing an answer, check it fits the grammar of the note. If it doesn't, you likely misheard the word class, not just the word.

📖 Reading — Passage 3

Survival tactics

  • Budget exactly 20 minutes for Passage 3. If Passages 1–2 ran over, cut this one to 17–18 min and guess the rest — never let it eat your whole remaining time.
  • Read only the first and last sentence of each paragraph before answering anything — that's usually enough to know its role, which is most of the work for Matching Headings.
  • True/False/Not Given: "False" means the text directly contradicts the statement. "Not Given" means the text is simply silent — don't use outside knowledge to decide.
  • Underline qualifiers in the question (all, most, some, always, may) — mismatches on these words are the single most common Passage 3 trap.
  • Questions follow the passage in order. Use that to narrow your search instead of re-reading from the top each time.

Practice drill — Circadian Rhythms and the Cost of the Nine-to-Five

Select any text in the passage or the questions below to highlight it — just like the real computer-delivered test. Click a highlight once to arm it (it turns red), then click it again to remove it.

A For most of the industrial era, the working day has begun at roughly the same hour regardless of who is doing the work. The nine-to-five schedule was not derived from any study of human biology; it was set by factory whistles and, later, office convention, and has simply persisted. Yet a substantial body of research now suggests that individuals differ predictably in when their bodies are naturally primed for alertness — a trait researchers call chronotype — and that forcing everyone onto an identical clock carries a real, measurable cost.

B At the centre of this system sits the suprachiasmatic nucleus, a cluster of neurons in the brain that governs the timing of hormones such as cortisol and melatonin, effectively setting the body's internal clock roughly a day in length. Twin studies suggest that around half of the variation in chronotype between individuals is genetic, with the remainder shaped by age, light exposure and habit. Adolescents in particular undergo a well-documented shift toward later chronotypes during puberty, a change that reverses only gradually in early adulthood.

C When an individual's biological clock is out of step with the schedule imposed on them, the result has been termed "social jet lag" — a chronic mismatch, rather than the acute kind experienced after a long flight. Unlike ordinary tiredness, social jet lag accumulates: sufferers do not simply feel sleepy, they build up a sleep debt linked in the literature to elevated risks of cardiovascular disease, metabolic disruption and depressed mood, even after controlling for total hours slept.

D Despite this evidence, flexible scheduling remains the exception rather than the rule in most workplaces. Employers frequently cite the coordination costs of staggered hours — meetings, client calls and shift handovers all become harder to plan when staff are not present at the same time. For organisations built around synchronous collaboration, the perceived administrative burden of accommodating chronotype has, in practice, outweighed the productivity gains that the research points to.

E A small number of schools and employers have nonetheless experimented with later start times. Several secondary schools that delayed their first lesson by an hour reported measurable improvements in test scores and a drop in recorded absences within a single academic year. Comparable pilot programmes in office settings found reduced reported fatigue among staff, though the same organisations noted that shift-based industries — manufacturing, healthcare, transport — cannot adopt these changes without redesigning rosters from the ground up, and public transport timetables rarely adjust to match.

F Advances in remote and asynchronous work tools have made chronotype-aware scheduling more feasible than at any point in the past, allowing some employees to shift their hours without a formal policy change. Yet flexibility of this kind introduces its own difficulties: those working far outside standard hours report greater isolation from colleagues, and the boundary between work and rest can blur when no schedule is imposed at all. No single arrangement yet reconciles biological variation with the practical demands of collective work.

✍️ Writing — Task 1 & Task 2

Survival tactics

  • One hour, two tasks: give Task 1 (150+ words) about 20 minutes and Task 2 (250+ words) about 40 — Task 2 counts for twice as much of the Writing band.
  • Task 2 structure examiners can follow instantly: intro (paraphrase + your clear position) → two body paragraphs (one idea each: topic sentence, explanation, example) → conclusion (restate position, no new ideas).
  • Answer every part of the question. A fluent essay that ignores half a two-part prompt is capped on Task Achievement regardless of how good the language is.
  • The 6→7.5 gap is usually Grammatical Range: aim for at least one genuine complex sentence per paragraph (a relative clause, a conditional) — but only where it reads naturally.
  • Drop memorised template phrases ("In today's modern world...", "There are many pros and cons...") — examiners flag them instantly. Plain, specific sentences score higher.
  • For Task 1: open with an overview sentence naming the 1–2 biggest overall trends before any numbers. Most candidates skip this — it's free Task Achievement marks.

🗣️ Speaking — Parts 1–3

Survival tactics

  • Part 1 (4–5 min, personal questions): answer directly, then add one reason or example in 2–3 sentences total — never a single word, never a speech.
  • Part 2 (cue card): 1 minute to prepare, up to 2 minutes to speak. Treat the four bullet prompts as your outline — a sentence or two per bullet paces you to fill the full 2 minutes without stalling or stopping early.
  • Part 3 (abstract discussion): extend every answer — reason, example, then a contrast or speculation ("...whereas in the past...", "...though that might change if..."). One-line answers are the single biggest Part 3 score killer.
  • Fluency is scored on natural self-correction and linking, not on speaking without a single pause — correcting yourself briefly ("she go— she went") and moving on reads as more fluent than freezing.
  • Record yourself on your phone during practice and listen back once. Most candidates don't notice their own filler-word habits (um, like, you know) until they hear the recording.
